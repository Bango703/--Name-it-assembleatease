TV Over the Fireplace: What It Really Costs and Takes

The heat check, the right height, the right mount and hidden cables, with every price up front, so the TV over your mantel looks built in.

By AssembleAtEase Editorial Team · Updated Oct 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Quick answer

Mounting a TV over a fireplace starts at $99 for the TV, plus $175 for the above-fireplace placement. Brick adds $110, and hiding the cords starts at $89. You see the exact total before you book and pay once the job is done.

The fireplace wall faces every seat in the room, so it is where most people picture the TV. Done well, it looks built in. Done badly, it hangs too high, runs too warm and trails cables down the brick.

This guide covers the checks to make before you buy a mount, what the visit involves and what changes the price, so you can decide whether the fireplace wall is right or whether a side wall works better.

Check the heat before anything else

TV manuals list a maximum operating temperature, commonly 104 degrees Fahrenheit. Run the fireplace for an hour, then hold your hand flat on the wall where the bottom of the TV would hang. If the wall is uncomfortable to touch, it is too warm for a screen.

A deep mantel pushes rising heat away from the wall and often makes the spot workable. A fireplace you rarely use, or a gas insert that sends most of its heat forward, changes the picture too. If the wall stays hot, a side wall is the better choice.

  • Run the fireplace for an hour before deciding
  • Feel the wall where the bottom of the TV would sit
  • Look up the TV's maximum operating temperature in its manual
  • Measure how far the mantel sticks out from the wall

How high the TV should sit

For comfortable viewing, the middle of the screen sits close to seated eye level, around 40 to 45 inches from the floor. Above a fireplace the bottom of the TV often starts higher than that, so the picture sits above the ideal line. A tilting mount angles the screen down toward the seats, and a pull-down mount lowers the TV to eye level while you watch.

Before booking, tape a paper outline of the TV on the wall at the height the mantel allows and sit on the couch for a few minutes. It is a cheap test and it settles most debates in the room.

Choosing the mount

You provide the mount. It should be rated for the weight and size of your TV and match the VESA hole pattern on its back. Above a mantel, the choice usually comes down to four types.

  • Tilting: the most common choice above a mantel; it angles the screen down a few degrees
  • Full-motion: swings the TV toward different seats and pulls it away from the wall
  • Pull-down: lowers the TV in front of the mantel to eye level, then lifts it back
  • Fixed: sits closest to the wall and suits a TV that can hang low enough

Brick, stone and what is inside the wall

Many fireplace walls are brick, stone or tile rather than drywall. These surfaces hold a mount well once it is fixed with masonry anchors, but drilling takes longer and needs different bits, which is why brick or concrete adds $110 and tile adds $65. Anchors for the surface come with the mount or are bought separately; the pro brings the drills and bits.

Behind drywall above a fireplace there may be a chimney chase, a metal flue or framing that differs from a normal wall. The pro checks for studs and obstacles before drilling and confirms the mounting points with you.

Hiding the cables

Cables running down the face of a fireplace are the detail people notice most. A surface cord cover is the simplest fix at $89 for up to six feet. In-wall cord concealment is $199 in drywall and $249 in brick or concrete, but a fireplace wall can rule it out: a firebox, a flue or solid masonry may leave no clear path. The pro checks the wall first and tells you which option fits.

Power is a separate question. Concealment kits carry the TV's cord down to an existing outlet lower on the wall. Adding a new outlet behind the TV is electrical work, and a licensed electrician handles it.

  • Surface cord cover: quickest, and it can be painted to match the wall
  • In-wall concealment: the cleanest look, if the wall has a clear path
  • A new outlet behind the TV: a licensed electrician handles it

What the job costs

Mounting is priced by TV size, then add-ons for the wall and placement. A 65 inch TV above a brick fireplace with a surface cord cover would be $149 for the TV, $175 above the fireplace, $110 for brick and $89 for the cord cover, before tax.

  • TV up to 40 inches: $99
  • TV 41 to 55 inches: $119
  • TV 56 to 65 inches: $149
  • TV 66 to 75 inches: $189
  • TV 76 to 85 inches: $229
  • Above fireplace add-on: $175
  • Brick or concrete add-on: $110

What to have ready for the visit

A cleared mantel and a decided height make the visit quick. Everything that came with the TV and the mount should be in the room.

  • The TV, its remote and the screws from its stand
  • The mount with all of its hardware, ideally unopened
  • The mantel cleared of decor and the floor in front of it clear
  • The taped outline still on the wall, or a decided height
  • Any streaming box you want connected at the same visit

Frequently asked questions

Is it a good idea to mount a TV above a fireplace?

It works when the wall stays within the TV maker's temperature limit and the screen can sit low enough to watch comfortably. If the wall gets hot when the fireplace runs, a side wall is the better spot.

Does a gas fireplace make a difference?

Gas fireplaces vary. Some send most of their heat into the room, others warm the wall above. Run it for an hour and feel the wall where the TV would hang.

Can the cables go inside a brick fireplace wall?

Sometimes. In-wall concealment through brick or concrete is $249 when there is a clear path. Solid masonry or a flue can rule it out, and a surface cord cover at $89 is the alternative.

Do you supply the TV mount?

No. You provide a mount rated for your TV and wall, and the pro brings the tools to install it.

How high should a TV be above a mantel?

As low as the mantel and the heat allow. When the TV has to sit high, a tilting or pull-down mount brings the picture closer to seated eye level.
