# Step down the take on big quotes

Keep the 30% take on small custom quotes. Step it down on big ones. Change how Easers bid. Today every quote adds 43 cents to each dollar of the Easer's bid, so the customer pays about 1.55 times the bid, tax included, at every job size. No competitor found does that on large work. Airtasker caps its customer fee, eBay and Upwork step their rates down, and lead sites like Thumbtack keep only a few percent of a big job. The recommended fee keeps 30% on the first $500 of a bid. It adds 25 cents per dollar from $500 to $1,500 and 15 cents per dollar above that, with a $45 minimum, and the owner may cut any quote to a floor of 15% of the subtotal. The owner's $1,200 example falls from $1,855.72 to $1,720.09, and a $3,000 bid falls from $4,639.28 to $3,993.34. The platform still keeps $339 and $573 after card fees, many times the roughly $16-$35 a job costs to run (estimate). The bigger lever is the bid. A $1,200 bid on a playset or gazebo is already a full market price before AAE adds anything, so Easers should bid labor-only take-home pay and state hours and crew size. Catalog pricing should not change: it sits at or below Best Buy and IKEA anchors and clears the cost floor even at the $129 minimum. One open question could move every price by 8.25%: TV mounting and other residential installation may not be taxable in Texas, and that needs a written CPA or Comptroller answer.

*How to read the numbers: "estimate" means my own calculation from cited inputs. "Unverified" means a third-party figure the company itself does not publish. AAE figures use the formula in the brief (customer subtotal = bid / 0.7), 8.25% Texas sales tax, and Stripe's 2.9% + 30 cents on the tax-inclusive total.*

## AssembleAtEase sits at the top of the market and never steps down

"Take" here means the share of the customer's pre-tax price that the platform keeps. AAE keeps 30% of the subtotal. On a custom quote that is the same as adding 42.9% on top of the Easer's bid. Competitors fall into two groups. **Managed marketplaces** set or hold the price, take the payment and stand behind the job. **Lead sites** sell a customer's contact details to pros and never touch the money.

|---|---|---|

TaskRabbit no longer publishes the percentages ([TaskRabbit Support](https://support.taskrabbit.com/hc/en-us/articles/204411610-What-s-the-Taskrabbit-Service-Fee-)). Among managed platforms, 30% is high but normal. William Blair's 2024 study of 319 marketplaces found an **average take of 19.4% and a median of 15%** ([William Blair](https://williamblair.com/-/media/downloads/eqr/2025/williamblair_whats-it-take-proprietary-study.pdf)). Its home-services group averages 15.0%, but real-estate sites at 2-5% drag that number down. The labor-heavy names appear to sit at about **25-35%**, with TaskRabbit near 30%, Rover near 31% and Wag near 35%. That label-to-bar mapping comes from the PDF's text order and should be checked visually. Uber, which also sets the price, kept **30.3%** in Q4 2024 ([Uber Q4 2024](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250205669831/en)). The pattern is simple. Platforms that price, dispatch, hold the money and stand behind the work earn high teens to low 30s. Lead sites earn far less, because the pro owns the customer after the first contact. AAE belongs in the first group, and 30% is defensible on small jobs while AAE delivers the full package.

What AAE lacks is a step-down. eBay charges **13.6% up to $7,500 and 2.35% above it** ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/help/selling-fees/selling/selling-fees?id=4822)). Upwork long charged 20% on the first $500, 10% up to $10,000 and 5% beyond (third-party sources) ([GigRadar](https://gigradar.io/blog/upwork-fees)). Rover caps its 11% owner fee at about $50 (community-reported) ([Rover community](https://www.rover.com/community/question/51846/what-are-rover-fees-for-owners)). Fiverr does the reverse at the bottom and adds a $3.50 fee on small orders ([Flexiple](https://flexiple.com/alternatives/flexiple-vs-fiverr)). The industry design is consistent: charge more on small tickets to cover fixed costs, and less on big tickets so the dollar cut does not push people away. Bill Gurley put the danger plainly: "The most dangerous strategy for any platform company is to price too high," and high rakes "create a natural impetus for suppliers to look elsewhere" ([Above the Crowd](https://abovethecrowd.com/2013/04/18/a-rake-too-far-optimal-platformpricing-strategy/)). On a job where the pro nets $2,100, Airtasker would keep roughly 14-22% of the customer's spend (estimate, built on unverified fees). AAE keeps 30%.

## Customers pay about 10% more for trust, not 55%

Customers do pay for trust, but not much more. In a late-2025 survey of 1,000+ homeowners, **72% said they would pay up to 10% more** for a contractor with a better service reputation ([Contractor Magazine](https://www.contractormag.com/around-the-web/news/55340108/homeowners-prioritize-experience-over-price-alone-new-housecall-pro-study-finds)). A Checkr survey found 67% would pay "a premium" for a background-checked worker, but it did not size the premium, and Checkr sells background checks ([Checkr](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/9-in-10-consumers-would-rather-wait-than-hire-without-verified-credentials)). The 2026 Housecall Pro survey found that **68% of homeowners get more quotes when a price comes in higher than expected** ([Housecall Pro](https://www.housecallpro.com/resources/home-service-spending-report-2026-release/)). The December 2025 survey found 97% expect clear pricing before they commit. On big jobs, most people already plan to compare: 67% of roofing customers seek three quotes ([Roofing Contractor](https://digitaledition.roofingcontractor.com/may-2024/homeowner-survey/)). No survey measured quote shopping on small assembly jobs.

So the 43% markup cannot be sold as a trust premium. The evidence supports about 10%. The markup works only when the customer never sees a cheaper version of the same job. In practice the customer compares AAE's total with the market price, not with the Easer's bid. That puts the weight on what the Easer bids.

Austin's own rates show where the line sits. TaskRabbit's November 2026 guides put Austin Tasker rates at **$43 an hour for furniture assembly, $61 for TV mounting and $64 for general handyman work**, with average invoices of $158, $131 and $200. Taskers keep all of it ([TaskRabbit furniture](https://www.taskrabbit.com/cost-guides/assemble-furniture); [TaskRabbit TV](https://www.taskrabbit.com/cost-guides/wall-mount-tv); [TaskRabbit handyman](https://www.taskrabbit.com/cost-guides/general-handyman)). The customer pays TaskRabbit's fees on top. Mr. Handyman of South Austin lists **$106 an hour** with a one-hour minimum, and that listing may be old ([Thumbtack](https://thumbtack.com/tx/austin/handyman/mr-handyman-south-austinlakeway/service/543786077129392141)). If an Easer bids $50 an hour take-home, AAE's 30% makes it $71.43 an hour before tax. That is close to TaskRabbit's handyman rate once its fee is added (about $74 at an unverified 15%), and well under the franchise. At $75 an hour take-home, AAE's price reaches **$107 an hour, the franchise rate**. That number becomes the bid guard in the recommendation. One more wrinkle: competitors usually quote before tax. AAE's quoted total includes 8.25%, so at a glance it reads about 8% higher.

The cash cost of a job is small next to a 30% take. The risk sits in the rare bad events.

Added up, a job costs about **$3-$8 in cash and $16-$35 fully loaded** (estimate). That supports a minimum platform margin of about **$25-$35 per job after card fees**. No public benchmark gives this floor; it is derived from the costs above. Card fees eat about 10.5-12% of AAE's 30%. Part of that is processing on sales tax AAE passes straight to the state.

Big jobs change the risk, not the running cost. One lost dispute on a $3,993 quote costs the platform about $4,139: the amount, two $15 fees and the $116 card fee. That wipes out the margin on about seven such jobs (estimate), and more if the Easer was already paid. Large quotes therefore need written scope, before-and-after photos and customer sign-off before the card is charged. The dollar margin must still grow with job size, and in the recommended schedule it does: $57, $153, $339 and $573 after card fees on bids of $150, $400, $1,200 and $3,000. Customer acquisition cost is left out of all of this. Counting it would raise the floor for first-time customers.

## One all-in price is right, and the tax line may be the real overcharge

AAE already shows the right format: one service subtotal, tax on its own line, and a total. Do not add a "platform fee" line. The research on itemized fees points one way. A meta-analysis of 43 studies found customers judge a separate charge kindly only when it carries a clear benefit and is normal for the category ([EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/when-does-partitioned-pricing-lead-to-more-favorable-consumer-preferences-meta-analytic-evidence-abraham-ajay/10011957976)). A line worth 30% of the price with no benefit attached fails both tests. Hiding fees until checkout does raise sales. In StubHub's randomized test, buyers who saw fees only at checkout **spent about 21% more and were 14% more likely to buy** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186.pdf)). But a quote is the commitment, so there is no later step to hide a fee in. Customers who choose under dripped fees also end up dissatisfied ([Marketing Science](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:39:y:2020:i:1:p:188-210)). Framing effects like these run in the single to low double digits. They cannot cover a 40-55% gap with the market.

The legal picture supports the same format. The FTC's fee rule took effect **May 12, 2025, and covers only live-event tickets and short-term lodging** ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-10/html/2024-30293.htm)). It still sends a signal: vague labels like "service fee" are barred there unless explained, with penalties up to $51,744 per violation ([Barnes & Thornburg](https://btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/ftc-finalizes-junk-fees-rule-new-pricing-disclosure-requirements-take-effect-may-12-2025)). California's SB 478 has required all-in prices since July 1, 2024 ([CA AG FAQ](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/SB%20478%20FAQ%20%28B%29.pdf)). TaskRabbit now shows a single Service Fee in California and Massachusetts, citing changes in state law ([TaskRabbit Support](https://support.taskrabbit.com/hc/articles/46260504648731)). No general Texas all-in pricing law was found. The 2025 bills targeted restaurants, landlords and tickets. A December 2025 Texas Attorney General "junk fee" deal was reported, but its details were behind a paywall ([Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2421238)). The federal FTC Act and the Texas DTPA still make a hidden-then-added fee risky.

Texas does set rules for showing tax. The Comptroller says the seller "must separately state the sales tax amount" unless it states that tax is included and posts a sign ([Texas Comptroller](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/sales/faq/collection.php)). Tax may not be rounded off ([Ezel summary of Rule 3.286](https://ezel.ai/tax-rulings/tx/9010l1048b11-tax-included-contract-bill-invoice-tax-must-be-separately-stated-to)). Rounding the subtotal to whole dollars is fine, as long as tax is then worked out exactly.

The bigger issue is whether some of the tax is owed at all. Old Comptroller rulings treat **furniture assembly as taxable**, even on furniture the customer owns ([Ezel 8712L0883E11](https://ezel.ai/tax-rulings/tx/8712l0883e11-furniture-equipment-assembly-disassembly-reassembly-into-new)). But labor to repair or remodel *residential* real property is generally not taxable ([Comptroller Pub. 94-116](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/publications/94-116.php)). Installing an item the installer did not sell was held not taxable in 1994 ([Ezel 9409L1315A03](https://ezel.ai/tax-rulings/tx/9409l1315a03-installation-charge-only-third-party-installation-can-be-a-taxable)). No ruling on TV wall mounting was found. The rulings are 25-35 years old. If TV mounting, smart-device installs or anchored outdoor structures turn out to be non-taxable, AAE is charging those customers 8.25% too much and building up a refund-or-remittance liability. A correct answer here could cut those totals by 8.25% without costing the platform a dollar of margin. It needs a CPA or a Comptroller private letter ruling.

## Big quotes leave $1,000-plus on the table for a side deal

The "gap" is the money between what the customer pays and what the Easer receives. It is the pot a customer and an Easer could split by going around the platform (disintermediation).

| Easer bid | Gap today | Gap, recommended |
|---|---|---|
| $150 | $81.97 | $81.66 |
| $400 | $218.57 | $218.11 |
| $1,200 | $655.72 | $520.09 |
| $3,000 | $1,639.28 | $993.34 |

The gap includes tax, which a cash side deal may skip (inference). The best direct study is reassuring for small jobs. It tracked 5,391 cleaners by GPS on an app that took 20-40%, and found that **no more than one in 83 relationships ended in a side deal** ([Kellogg working paper](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/bray/doc/disintermediation/disintermediation.pdf)). The authors credit three things: the value of safety when a stranger enters the home, an average job of only $31.58, and the fact that 90% of pairs met only once. AAE's catalog work fits that profile.

Big custom quotes do not. They carry hundreds of dollars of gap, and they often lead to a second project. A randomized study on a freelance marketplace found that trust built by the platform increases side deals once relationships form ([Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3583)). Cutting off a cheap outside channel reduced them by **about 18%** ([Management Science, 2024](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:70:y:2024:i:11:p:7868-7891)). AAE already blocks contact-sharing and alerts the owner. The step-down adds a price lever on top: it removes about $646 of the pot on a $3,000 bid. Upwork and Airtasker both charge less on repeat relationships for the same reason ([GigRadar](https://gigradar.io/blog/upwork-fees); [Airtasker](https://airtasker.com/uk/cancellation-policy)). For AAE, a repeat customer's custom quote with the same Easer is a good use of the owner's floor.

## The recommendation: three steps, a $45 minimum, a 15% floor

### The fee rules

| Rule | Setting |
|---|---|
| First $500 of the Easer's bid | Add 43 cents per dollar. This is the same 30% take as the catalog. |
| Bid dollars from $500 to $1,500 | Add 25 cents per dollar (20% of that slice of the subtotal). |
| Bid dollars above $1,500 | Add 15 cents per dollar (about 13% of that slice). |
| Minimum platform fee | $45 per quote. The quote subtotal is never below the catalog minimum ($129 Austin, $149 elsewhere). |
| Owner adjustment | The owner may lower the fee on any quote to a floor: the larger of $45 or 15% of the subtotal. The schedule is the ceiling. Every cut below the schedule records a reason. |
| Rounding | Round the subtotal down to whole dollars. The platform absorbs the cents. Easer pay never changes. Tax is calculated exactly. |
| Display | One service subtotal, tax on its own line, one total. No fee line. |
| Easer pay | Exactly the bid. |

The steps work like tax brackets. Each rate applies only to its slice, so there is no cliff where a bigger bid suddenly costs less. The platform's share falls slowly: 31% on a $100 bid (the minimum applies), 30% at $500, 26.5% at $800, 23.6% at $1,500, 21.2% at $2,000 and 16.5% at $5,000. Those numbers sit near Airtasker's lower-volume tiers. They leave AAE at the top of the managed range without putting it above the range on big jobs.

### How Easers should bid

Easers bid the take-home pay they want for the labor. Hours times people times their hourly rate. No materials, no fee, no tax: AAE adds those. The bid form asks for estimated hours and crew size, so the owner can see the bid per person-hour. Austin Taskers keep $43-$64 an hour, so $45-$65 a person-hour is competitive. The owner should question any bid above **$75 per person-hour**, the point where AAE's price passes the $106 franchise rate. That is guidance, never a required rate. Easers stay independent and set their own bids, and the owner can decline a bid. The Angi Services settlement is the warning on the other side. The FTC and New York fined Handy $2.95 million over inflated "up to" pay claims and fees charged to pros without consent ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2025/attorney-general-james-and-ftc-secure-295-million-angi-services-misleading)). AAE should promise Easers exactly one thing: the bid is the pay.

### What it takes to build

Today's split code accepts only a 30% or a 25% fee. The function `normalizePlatformFeePct` in `api/_source-of-truth.js` quietly turns any other percentage into 30%. The stepped fee cannot be typed in as a percentage. It has to be stored on each quote as a dollar fee, with Easer pay fixed at the bid. That file is a critical module under Article 10. The change needs explicit authorization, audit-before-code, and tests proving three things: the Easer is paid exactly the bid, the fee never falls below the floor, and tax is computed exactly. Before a quote goes out, the quote screen should show the owner the fee, the share, and the margin after card fees.

### What would change the recommendation

Real quote data matters most. If more than half of quotes sent at the default schedule are accepted, raise the top step. If fewer than a third are accepted, and lost quotes cite price, use the floor more and lower the middle step. Those thresholds are judgment, not benchmarks. A CPA answer that TV mounting and installation are non-taxable would cut those totals by 8.25% and leave room to keep the steps where they are. A real damage or dispute rate on large jobs above about 2% would justify a higher floor. Side deals showing up on repeat pairs would justify a standing repeat-customer rate. A current TaskRabbit or Airtasker invoice would firm up the competitor numbers, most of which are unverified.

## Conclusion

The take rate is the smallest of three price levers. The bid definition decides whether a quote lands in the market at all. A labor-only bid keeps even today's formula competitive, and a retail-level bid sinks any formula. The tax answer could cut some totals by 8.25% at no cost to margin. The step-down then trims 7-14% off big quotes and shrinks the side-deal pot by up to $646 on a $3,000 bid. Judge large jobs in dollars rather than percentages. Dollars are what the platform banks ($339 and $573 after card fees), and dollars are what it stands to lose in a single dispute.

Chairman's ruling: adopt it. Leave the catalog alone. Change the Easer bid form first, adding take-home pay, hours, crew size and the $75 per person-hour check, because it costs nothing and fixes most of the overpricing. Build the stepped quote fee properly in the split code, with the owner floor, the logged reason and the three tests, because it touches a critical module. Send the tax question to a CPA now, and keep collecting 8.25% until a written answer arrives, since under-collecting a taxable service is the costlier mistake (judgment). Review after the first 10 quotes and move the steps on real acceptance data, not on this report.
